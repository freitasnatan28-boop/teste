## Descrição

Greenland says it's "not for sale". But buying land is exactly how the United States was built. From Napoleon's
fire sale of Louisiana to the "icebox" Russia sold for 2 cents an acre, and a secret 1946 offer of $100 million in
gold, here is every deal behind the map, and why Greenland breaks the pattern.

## Roteiro

### 1. Not For Sale

**[VISUAL]** Globe zooms into Greenland. Trump portrait, speech bubble "LET'S BUY IT". Denmark flag replies.
In 2019, the President of the United States made an offer that sounded like a joke. He wanted to buy Greenland. The biggest island on Earth. Denmark's prime minister called the idea absurd. And Greenland's answer was even shorter: we are not for sale.

**[VISUAL]** "2025" on top. Greenland pulses.
Then, in 2025, he brought it up again. And this time, nobody was laughing.

**[VISUAL]** Map of the United States. Purchased territories light up one by one.
But here's what most people miss. This isn't a crazy new idea. Buying land is how America was built. Close to forty percent of the United States wasn't conquered. It was bought. With cash, with gold, and sometimes with a deal so lopsided it still doesn't feel real.

**[VISUAL]** Title over the map.
So why does America keep trying to buy Greenland? To answer that, we need to look at every deal that came before it. Because every single one of them follows the same pattern.

### 2. The Deal of the Century

**[VISUAL]** "1803". The young United States along the Atlantic. Mississippi River. Pin on New Orleans.
In 1803, the United States was a young country squeezed along the Atlantic coast. Its western border was the Mississippi River. And at the mouth of that river sat one city that controlled everything: New Orleans. Farmers in Kentucky and Ohio shipped their crops down the river and out through that one port.

**[VISUAL]** Louisiana painted with the French flag. Napoleon portrait.
The problem? New Orleans, and the huge territory behind it, now belonged to France. And France was ruled by Napoleon Bonaparte.

**[VISUAL]** Jefferson portrait. Ship route to Paris.
President Thomas Jefferson sent his envoys to Paris with a simple mission: buy New Orleans. He authorized them to spend up to ten million dollars, just for the city and a strip of coast.

**[VISUAL]** Camera to Haiti. Explosion icon. French army defeated.
But Napoleon had a problem of his own. His army had just been crushed in Haiti, where enslaved people had risen up and defeated one of the strongest armies in Europe. Without Haiti and its sugar, his American empire made no sense. And a new war with Britain was coming. He needed money. Fast.

**[VISUAL]** Back to Paris. Napoleon bubble: "WHY NOT ALL OF IT?"
So the French made an offer the Americans never expected. Not just the city. All of Louisiana.

**[VISUAL]** Louisiana Purchase fills with the US flag. Price tag counts up to $15,000,000.
The price: fifteen million dollars, for about eight hundred and twenty-eight thousand square miles. Roughly three cents an acre. Overnight, the United States doubled in size. Today, that land is all or part of fifteen states.

**[VISUAL]** Money route London and Amsterdam to Paris. British flag.
And here's the twist. The deal was financed with bonds sold through British and Dutch banks. So in a way, British money helped pay Napoleon, right before he went back to war against Britain.

### 3. Florida, Mexico and a Desert

**[VISUAL]** "1819". Florida with the Spanish flag. Price tag "$0".
The next deal came in 1819. Spain owned Florida, but it could barely control it. So Spain handed it over. The surprising part? Spain didn't get a single dollar. The United States simply agreed to pay up to five million dollars in claims that American citizens had against Spain.

**[VISUAL]** "1846". Mexico's flag over the Southwest. US units move in.
Then came the hardest case. In 1846, the United States went to war with Mexico. When it ended two years later, Mexico gave up more than half a million square miles: today's California, Nevada, Utah, and most of Arizona. The United States paid fifteen million dollars for it.

**[VISUAL]** Zoom to California. Gold icon on Sutter's Mill.
Was it a purchase, or a price tag stapled to a conquest? Historians still argue. But one detail stands out. Nine days before the treaty was signed, a worker found gold in a California river. Neither government knew yet. Within a few years, a single year of California gold was worth more than the entire price of the deal.

**[VISUAL]** "1853". Gadsden strip lights up. Railroad line to the Pacific.
Five years later, America came back for one more strip of desert. Ten million dollars for southern Arizona and New Mexico. Why? A railroad. Engineers wanted the flattest route to the Pacific, and it ran through Mexico. But the strangest deal of all was still to come, in the frozen north.

### 4. Seward's Icebox

**[VISUAL]** Camera flies north. Alaska painted with the Russian flag. Tsar portrait.
Now jump to the far north. In the 1860s, Alaska belonged to the Russian Empire. But Russia had a problem. It had just lost the Crimean War against Britain and France. Its treasury was strained, and Alaska was almost impossible to defend.

**[VISUAL]** British Canada next door, in red. Warning icon.
Right next door sat British Canada. If another war broke out, Britain could simply take Alaska. So the Tsar made a cold calculation: better to sell it to America than lose it to Britain for nothing.

**[VISUAL]** "1867". Seward portrait. Alaska turns into the US flag. Price tag $7,200,000.
In 1867, Secretary of State William Seward signed the deal. Seven point two million dollars. About two cents an acre, for a territory more than twice the size of Texas. The canceled check is still kept at the National Archives.

**[VISUAL]** Critics' bubbles: "SEWARD'S FOLLY!", "AN ICEBOX!"
Critics mocked it. They called it Seward's Folly. Seward's Icebox. Why pay for a frozen wasteland?

**[VISUAL]** Gold rush photo. Oil icon at Prudhoe Bay. Bering Strait, Soviet flag a few miles away.
Then came gold. Then, in 1968, oil at Prudhoe Bay, the largest oil field ever found in North America. And during the Cold War, Alaska became America's front line, just a few miles from Soviet territory across the Bering Strait. Nobody calls it folly anymore.

### 5. Islands for Gold

**[VISUAL]** "1917". Caribbean. Danish West Indies with Denmark's flag. Panama Canal route.
In 1917, during World War One, America bought land from Denmark for the first time. Washington feared Germany could grab the Danish West Indies, islands sitting right on the shipping lanes to the brand new Panama Canal.

**[VISUAL]** Price tag $25,000,000 IN GOLD. Islands turn American.
The price: twenty-five million dollars in gold. Today, we call them the US Virgin Islands. And it was not the last time America knocked on Denmark's door.

### 6. The Secret Offer

**[VISUAL]** "1946". Greenland. Truman portrait. Price tag $100,000,000 IN GOLD.
In 1946, right after World War Two, the Truman administration made Denmark a secret offer: one hundred million dollars, in gold, for Greenland.

**[VISUAL]** Denmark bubble "NO". "SECRET" stamp. "1991" declassified.
Denmark said no. And the offer stayed hidden for decades, until declassified documents surfaced in 1991.

**[VISUAL]** Radar icon at Pituffik, northwest Greenland. Missile routes over the pole.
But America got most of what it wanted anyway. Under a defense agreement with Denmark, it built a giant air base in the far north of Greenland. Today it's called Pituffik Space Base, and it watches the skies for missiles coming over the North Pole.

### 7. Why Greenland, Why Now

**[VISUAL]** Wide Arctic view. Russia, Greenland and the US. Shortest route over the top of the world.
So why does Greenland matter so much? Look at the top of the map. The shortest path between Russia and the United States runs over the Arctic, right past Greenland.

**[VISUAL]** Ice retreating. New shipping routes appear through the Arctic.
And the Arctic is changing. As the ice melts, new shipping routes are opening up, and the ground underneath is becoming easier to reach. Ships sailing between Asia and Europe could one day cut thousands of miles by going over the top of the world.

**[VISUAL]** Rare earths icon in southern Greenland. China flag, "90%".
Under that ground are some of the largest deposits of rare earth elements in the world. The metals inside your phone, your electric car, and a fighter jet. Right now, China processes around ninety percent of them. Greenland could be one way to break that grip. And America is not the only one looking. In 2018, a Chinese state-owned company bid to build airports in Greenland. Denmark stepped in and paid for them itself.

**[VISUAL]** Greenland: 56,000 people. Denmark's flag and money flowing in.
Greenland itself has only about fifty-six thousand people. And Denmark covers a big part of its budget every single year.

### 8. The Pattern

**[VISUAL]** Every territory lit up again, each with its seller's flag and a crisis icon.
So look at every deal again. Napoleon needed cash for a war. Spain couldn't hold Florida. Mexico had just lost a war. Russia was afraid of Britain. Denmark was afraid of Germany. Every time, America bought land from someone in trouble.

**[VISUAL]** Greenland flag. Greenlanders' bubble: "OUR CHOICE".
Greenland breaks the pattern. Denmark isn't desperate. And this time, the people who actually live there have a voice. Greenland runs most of its own affairs, and by law it has the right to choose independence.
