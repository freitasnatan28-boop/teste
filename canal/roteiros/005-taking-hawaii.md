## Descrição

Hawaii was an independent kingdom with a queen, a palace with electric lights and treaties with the great powers.
Then a group of American businessmen, backed by US Marines, took it over. Here is how sugar, a naval base and one
night in 1893 turned a kingdom into the 50th state, and why Congress apologized a century later.

## Roteiro

### 1. A Kingdom in the Pacific

**[VISUAL]** Globe spins to the middle of the Pacific. Hawaii lights up. Distance counter 2,400 MILES.
In the middle of the Pacific Ocean, more than two thousand miles from California, there is a chain of islands that used to be a country. It had a king, a flag, an army and treaties with the great powers of the world.

**[VISUAL]** Treaty icons: USA, Britain, France, Japan around Hawaii. Book icon.
The Kingdom of Hawaii had embassies abroad, a written constitution, and one of the highest literacy rates in the world.


**[VISUAL]** "1893". Queen portrait. Marines icon. Stamp "OVERTHROWN".
Then, in 1893, a small group of American businessmen, backed by United States Marines, removed its queen. Five years later, the islands belonged to the United States.

**[VISUAL]** "1993". Document: Apology. Title over the map.
A hundred years after that, the United States Congress did something it almost never does. It apologized. So how did America take Hawaii? It started, like so many stories on this channel, with geography.

### 2. The Crossroads of the Pacific

**[VISUAL]** Routes: San Francisco, Asia, Australia all passing near Hawaii.
Look where Hawaii sits. Halfway between America and Asia, alone in the biggest ocean on Earth. In the age of sailing ships and steamships, every long voyage across the Pacific needed a place to stop for water, food and coal. Hawaii was that place. Whaling ships from New England turned Honolulu and Lahaina into some of the busiest ports in the Pacific.

**[VISUAL]** "1778". Captain Cook route. British flag. Cook icon.
The British explorer James Cook reached the islands in 1778. Soon whalers, traders and missionaries followed, many of them from New England.

**[VISUAL]** "1779". Kealakekua Bay. Explosion icon.
A year after he arrived, Cook was killed in a fight with Hawaiians at Kealakekua Bay, on the Big Island.


**[VISUAL]** "1810". Kamehameha unites the islands. Hawaiian flag.
By 1810, a chief named Kamehameha had united all the islands into one kingdom. Its flag still flies today, with the British Union Jack in the corner and eight stripes, one for each main island.

**[VISUAL]** "1820". Missionary ship route from Boston to Honolulu. Book icon.
In 1820, the first American missionaries arrived from Boston. They built schools and churches, helped put the Hawaiian language into writing, and their families would soon become some of the most powerful people in the islands.


### 3. Sugar

**[VISUAL]** Sugar plantations icons. Ships to San Francisco. Price tags.
Then came sugar. The sons of the missionaries became planters and businessmen, and Hawaiian sugar made them rich. Their biggest market was the United States.

**[VISUAL]** Worker routes from China, Japan, Portugal, Philippines.
To work the fields, the planters brought in tens of thousands of laborers from China, Japan, Portugal and later the Philippines. Meanwhile, diseases brought by foreigners had devastated the Native Hawaiian population.

**[VISUAL]** Population counter falls: 300,000 to 40,000.
Before Cook arrived, there were probably around three hundred thousand Native Hawaiians. A century later, only about forty thousand were left. In their own country, they were becoming a minority.


**[VISUAL]** "1875". Treaty document. Duty-free sugar. Pearl Harbor pin.
In 1875, the kingdom signed a deal with Washington: Hawaiian sugar could enter the United States tax-free. A few years later, as part of renewing that deal, the United States got something it wanted badly: the right to use a harbor near Honolulu. Its name was Pearl Harbor.

### 4. The Bayonet Constitution

**[VISUAL]** "1887". King Kalakaua portrait. Bubble. Bayonet icon.
The king was David Kalakaua. He traveled around the world, put electric lights in his palace before the White House had them, and tried to keep his country independent. In 1881, he became the first reigning monarch in history to travel all the way around the world.

**[VISUAL]** Armed militia icon. Document with stamp "SIGN IT".
In 1887, a group of mostly American businessmen with an armed militia forced him to sign a new constitution. It stripped most of the king's power and gave the vote to wealthy foreigners, while most Asians and many poor Hawaiians lost theirs. People called it the Bayonet Constitution.

### 5. The Overthrow

**[VISUAL]** "1891". Queen Liliuokalani portrait. Iolani Palace photo.
In 1891, Kalakaua died, and his sister became queen. Her name was Liliuokalani. She wanted to restore power to the monarchy and the vote to her people.

**[VISUAL]** Music note icon. Bubble "ALOHA OE".
She was also a songwriter. Her song Aloha Oe is still one of the most famous Hawaiian songs ever written.


**[VISUAL]** "1893". Committee of Safety: 13 men. USS Boston route to Honolulu. Marines.
The businessmen moved first. A group of thirteen men called the Committee of Safety declared the queen's government over. And the American minister in Honolulu ordered about one hundred and sixty sailors and marines from the warship USS Boston to come ashore. The marines did not fire a shot. But they were posted near the palace, and everyone understood the message.

**[VISUAL]** Queen bubble "TO AVOID BLOODSHED". Stamp "OVERTHROWN".
The queen had soldiers loyal to her. But she did not want a war with the United States. To avoid bloodshed, she gave up her power, not to the businessmen, but to the United States, hoping Washington would put her back.

**[VISUAL]** Sanford Dole portrait. Republic of Hawaii flag. "1894".
A judge named Sanford Dole became the leader of the new government. His cousin would later found the Dole pineapple company.

### 6. An Act of War

**[VISUAL]** Cleveland portrait. Investigator icon. Report document. Stamp "BLOUNT REPORT".
At first, it almost worked. President Grover Cleveland sent an investigator, James Blount, to find out what happened. His conclusion: the overthrow could not have succeeded without the American marines.

**[VISUAL]** Cleveland bubble "AN ACT OF WAR". Queen portrait.
Cleveland called it an act of war against a friendly nation, and he asked for the queen to be restored. But the new government refused, and Congress would not act.

**[VISUAL]** "1895". Prison icon. Queen under arrest.
In 1895, supporters of the queen tried to take the country back. They failed. The queen was arrested and forced to give up the throne.

**[VISUAL]** Iolani Palace photo. Counter 8 MONTHS.
She spent about eight months as a prisoner in a single room of the palace where she had once ruled.


**[VISUAL]** Petition document. Counter 21,000 SIGNATURES.
When annexation came up again, Native Hawaiians fought back with paper. More than twenty-one thousand of them signed petitions against it. A treaty of annexation failed in the Senate.

**[VISUAL]** "1898". Warships to Manila. Hawaii as the stepping stone. US flag rises.
Then came the Spanish-American War. American warships were heading to the Philippines, and Hawaii was the perfect stop on the way. In 1898, Congress annexed the islands with a simple resolution. No treaty. No vote by the people who lived there. On August 12th, 1898, the Hawaiian flag was lowered at Iolani Palace and the American flag went up. Many Hawaiians stayed home that day, refusing to watch.

### 7. The Fiftieth State

**[VISUAL]** "1941". Pearl Harbor. Japanese planes route. Explosion icons.
Hawaii became the heart of American power in the Pacific. That is exactly why Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7th, 1941, pulling the United States into World War Two.

**[VISUAL]** Counter 2,403 KILLED. Battleship icons.
More than two thousand four hundred Americans were killed that morning.


**[VISUAL]** "1959". Star added to the flag. 50.
In 1959, Hawaii became the fiftieth state.

**[VISUAL]** Vote counter 94% YES. Question icon.
More than ninety percent of voters said yes. But the ballot only offered statehood or staying a territory. Independence was not on it.
 Today, the Pentagon runs its whole Indo-Pacific Command from Hawaii, watching over half the planet.

**[VISUAL]** "1993". Apology document. Clinton signs. Counter 100 YEARS.
And in 1993, on the hundredth anniversary of the overthrow, Congress passed a resolution, and President Bill Clinton signed it. It apologized to Native Hawaiians for the overthrow of their kingdom.

### 8. The Pattern

**[VISUAL]** Wide map: Hawaii, Panama, Greenland, Canada lit.
Look at the map one more time. Hawaii, the Panama Canal, Greenland, Canada. Islands and passages in the middle of the routes that move the world. Again and again, America has wanted the same thing: the stepping stones.

**[VISUAL]** Back to Hawaii. Hawaiian flag. Bubble "ALOHA".
Hawaii is the one place where it got all of it. A kingdom became a state. And the apology, a century later, shows that even the country that took it knows how it happened.

**[VISUAL]** Tourist plane routes. Counter 10 MILLION VISITORS. Sovereignty flag.
Today, around ten million tourists visit Hawaii every year. And a Native Hawaiian sovereignty movement still argues that the kingdom was never legally given up.
