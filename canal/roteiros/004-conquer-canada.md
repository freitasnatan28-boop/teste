the world's longest border.

## Roteiro

### 1. The 51st State

**[VISUAL]** "2025". Washington. Trump portrait, bubble "THE 51ST STATE". Canada lights up.
In 2025, the President of the United States started calling Canada something strange. The fifty-first state. Canada's leaders answered fast: Canada is not for sale, and it never will be.

**[VISUAL]** Border line glows from ocean to ocean. Counter 5,525 MILES.
It sounds like a joke between two friendly neighbors. They share the longest border on Earth, about five thousand five hundred miles, and almost no soldiers guard it.

**[VISUAL]** Canada huge on the map. Counters 40 MILLION vs 340 MILLION. Strip near the border lights up.
Canada is the second largest country on Earth, bigger than the entire United States. But it has only about forty million people, fewer than California. And most of them live within a hundred miles of the American border.


**[VISUAL]** Title over the map. Swords icon on the border.
But here is what most Americans never learned in school. The United States has tried to take Canada before. More than once. With real armies. And every single time, it went badly.

### 2. The Fourteenth Colony

**[VISUAL]** "1775". Thirteen colonies in blue. Quebec. Arnold and Montgomery routes north.
In 1775, the American Revolution had just begun, and the rebels had a bold idea. Canada should be the fourteenth colony. So two American armies marched north to take Quebec.

**[VISUAL]** Snowstorm icons. Quebec City. Explosion. Montgomery dead.
One army sailed up Lake Champlain. The other, led by Benedict Arnold, dragged boats through the forests of Maine. On the last night of 1775, they attacked Quebec City in a blizzard. The general leading the attack was killed. Arnold was shot in the leg. The invasion collapsed.

**[VISUAL]** Smallpox icons. British ships up the St. Lawrence. Retreat arrows south.
Smallpox tore through the American camps. In the spring of 1776, British reinforcements sailed up the Saint Lawrence River, and what was left of the American army retreated south.

**[VISUAL]** Arnold portrait. Stamp "TRAITOR".
And Benedict Arnold? A few years later, he switched sides and joined the British. In America, his name still means traitor.


**[VISUAL]** Document icon: Articles of Confederation. Stamp "OPEN INVITATION".
Even then, America did not give up the dream. Its first constitution, the Articles of Confederation, had a special rule: Canada could join the union whenever it wanted. No other colony got that offer. Canada never said yes.

**[VISUAL]** Loyalist routes north from New York and New England.
And when the Revolution ended, tens of thousands of Americans who had stayed loyal to the king fled north. Some of the people who built English Canada were Americans who refused to become American.


### 3. A Mere Matter of Marching

**[VISUAL]** "1812". Madison portrait. War declared. Arrows into Canada.
Then came 1812. The United States declared war on Britain, and the plan was simple: invade Canada while the British were busy fighting Napoleon in Europe.

**[VISUAL]** Jefferson bubble "A MERE MATTER OF MARCHING".
Thomas Jefferson predicted it would be easy. Taking Canada, he wrote, would be a mere matter of marching.

**[VISUAL]** Detroit. Surrender stamp. Arrows pushed back.
It was not. Two months later, an American general surrendered the entire city of Detroit without a fight. Attack after attack along the border failed.

**[VISUAL]** Tecumseh icon. Native allies alongside British flags.
The British also had powerful allies. The Shawnee leader Tecumseh and his confederacy fought on their side, hoping a British victory would stop American settlers from taking their land.


**[VISUAL]** York burns. Then Washington burns. White House icon on fire.
In 1813, American troops burned the town of York, today called Toronto. A year later, the British came for revenge. They marched into Washington and set fire to the Capitol and the White House. The president had to flee his own capital.

**[VISUAL]** Baltimore. Fort McHenry. Flag icon. Bombardment.
Weeks later, the British attacked Baltimore. They failed. An American lawyer who watched the bombardment wrote a poem about the flag still flying over the fort. It became the national anthem: The Star-Spangled Banner.


**[VISUAL]** "1814". Treaty document. Border unchanged. Price tag $0.
The war ended in a treaty that changed almost nothing. After two and a half years of fighting, the border stayed exactly where it was.

### 4. Fifty-Four Forty or Fight

**[VISUAL]** "1844". Oregon Country. Line at 54°40'. Polk portrait.
The next fight was over the Pacific Northwest. In 1844, American expansionists wanted everything up to the edge of Russian Alaska. Their slogan: fifty-four forty or fight.

**[VISUAL]** Line moves down to the 49th parallel. Treaty 1846.
But President Polk was already heading for war with Mexico. So in 1846 he signed a compromise with Britain, and the border was drawn along the forty-ninth parallel, where it still runs today.

**[VISUAL]** "1867". Canada becomes a country. Alaska bought the same year.
The fear of America also helped create modern Canada. In 1867, the British colonies united into one country, partly to stand together against their giant neighbor. That same year, the United States bought Alaska, and Canada found itself squeezed between American land on both sides.

**[VISUAL]** "1866". Fenian raid arrows from the US into Canada.
They had reason to worry. After the Civil War, thousands of Irish American veterans, called the Fenians, launched raids into Canada, hoping to trade it for Irish independence. They were beaten back, but the message was clear.


### 5. War Plan Red

**[VISUAL]** "1930". Secret document. Stamp "SECRET". Code name CRIMSON on Canada.
By the twentieth century, the two countries were friends. But in 1930, the US Army approved a secret plan for a war with the British Empire. It was called War Plan Red, and in it, Canada had a code name: Crimson.

**[VISUAL]** Attack arrows: Halifax, Montreal, Winnipeg, Vancouver.
The plan was to strike first. Seize the port of Halifax, cut the railways, take Montreal and Quebec, and grab the Great Lakes region before Britain could send help.

**[VISUAL]** Canada's own plan: arrows south to Seattle, Detroit, Albany. "1921" Buster Brown.
And Canada had a plan too. In 1921, a Canadian officer named James Sutherland Brown wrote Defence Scheme Number One: a surprise attack on American cities like Seattle, Detroit and Albany, to slow the Americans down.

**[VISUAL]** Plans filed away. Stamp "DECLASSIFIED 1974".
Neither plan was ever used. War Plan Red was withdrawn in 1939, when the real enemy turned out to be Nazi Germany, and it stayed secret until 1974.

**[VISUAL]** Poison gas icon. Airfields near the border. Stamp "1935".
Before that, an update in 1935 even approved the use of poison gas, and Congress paid for new airfields near the border, disguised as civilian airports.


### 6. Why Now

**[VISUAL]** Trade routes across the border. Price tag $909 BILLION.
Today, Canada and the United States trade more than nine hundred billion dollars a year. Cars, food, electricity, metals. Many products cross the border several times before they are finished.

**[VISUAL]** Ambassador Bridge, Detroit to Windsor. Trucks route. Electricity icon from Quebec to New York.
The Ambassador Bridge, between Detroit and Windsor, carries about a quarter of all the trade between the two countries. And dams in Quebec send electricity south to power homes in New York and New England.


**[VISUAL]** Oil pipelines from Alberta south. Oil icon. 60%.
And there is oil. Canada sends the United States more crude oil than any other country, around sixty percent of all the crude America imports.

**[VISUAL]** Arctic: Northwest Passage opening. Greenland, Alaska, Canada lit.
Then there is the Arctic. As the ice melts, a new shipping route is opening through Canada's northern islands. Canada says those waters are Canadian. Washington says they are international. Put Alaska, Canada and Greenland on one map, and you can see why the top of the world matters so much.

**[VISUAL]** Tariff icons. Canada flag. Bubble "NOT FOR SALE".
In 2025, the fifty-first state talk came with tariffs. Canadians booed the American anthem at hockey games, and many stopped buying American products. A friendship that survived two world wars suddenly felt very cold. When Canada's new prime minister visited the White House, he said it to the president's face: some places are never for sale.

### 7. The Pattern

**[VISUAL]** Wide map: Greenland, Canada, Panama lit. Arrows from Washington.
Look at the map again. Greenland. The Panama Canal. Canada. The same idea keeps coming back: more land, more resources, more control of the routes.

**[VISUAL]** Back to the border, peaceful. Canada flag and US flag side by side.
But Canada is the clearest lesson of all. America tried force, and it failed. What finally worked was the opposite: trade, trust, and an open border. Since 1814, the two countries have not fired a single shot at each other. Their soldiers fought side by side in two world wars, in Korea and in Afghanistan. Whether that lesson still holds is the real question.
