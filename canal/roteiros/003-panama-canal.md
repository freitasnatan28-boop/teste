## Descrição

America took a piece of Colombia, built the greatest shortcut on Earth, ran it like a little America for 85 years,
and then handed it over for nothing. Now Washington wants it back. Here is the whole story behind the map: the French
disaster, the revolution Teddy Roosevelt backed, the flag riots of 1964, the treaty that passed by one vote, and why
the canal matters more than ever.

## Roteiro

### 1. We're Taking It Back

**[VISUAL]** "2025". Washington. Trump portrait, bubble "WE'RE TAKING IT BACK". Camera flies south to Panama.
In January 2025, in his inaugural speech, the President of the United States made a promise that stunned an entire country. He said America was taking the Panama Canal back.

**[VISUAL]** Panama flag. Bubble "EVERY SQUARE METER IS OURS".
Panama's answer came fast. The canal belongs to Panama, every square meter of it.

**[VISUAL]** The canal lights up. Price tag "$0". "1999".
But here is the strange part. The United States built that canal. It ran it for eighty-five years. And then, in 1999, it handed the whole thing over. Panama did not pay a single cent.

**[VISUAL]** Title over the map.
So why would the most powerful country on Earth give away one of the most valuable pieces of land on the planet? And why does it want it back now? To understand that, we need to go back to a time when ships had to sail around an entire continent.

### 2. The Shortcut

**[VISUAL]** Route New York to San Francisco around Cape Horn. Distance counter 13,000 MILES.
Before the canal, a ship going from New York to San Francisco had to sail all the way around South America, past the storms of Cape Horn. About thirteen thousand miles.

**[VISUAL]** Zoom to the isthmus. Route through Panama. 5,000 MILES.
But in one place, the two oceans almost touch. A thin strip of jungle in Panama, about fifty miles wide. Cut through it, and the same trip drops to around five thousand miles. Whoever controlled that strip would control one of the most important shortcuts on Earth.

**[VISUAL]** "1534". Spain flag over the isthmus. Question mark icon.
The idea was not new. In 1534, the King of Spain ordered a survey to see if the isthmus could be cut. For almost four hundred years, nobody managed to do it.

### 3. The French Disaster

**[VISUAL]** "1881". Paris. De Lesseps portrait. Route Suez, then to Panama.
The first to try were the French. In 1881, Ferdinand de Lesseps, the hero who had built the Suez Canal, arrived in Panama with a bold plan: dig a canal at sea level, straight through the mountains.

**[VISUAL]** Jungle. Mosquito icons everywhere. Skull counter 22,000.
The jungle had other plans. Mudslides buried the work again and again. And then came the mosquitoes. Yellow fever and malaria tore through the camps. By most estimates, around twenty-two thousand workers died.

**[VISUAL]** "1889". Bankrupt stamp. Bubble from Paris.
In 1889, the French company went bankrupt, in one of the biggest financial scandals of the century. The great canal was left half-dug in the jungle, waiting for someone else.

### 4. How to Buy a Country

**[VISUAL]** "1903". Map: Panama is part of Colombia, painted with Colombia's flag. Roosevelt portrait.
That someone was Theodore Roosevelt. In 1903, Panama was not even a country. It was a province of Colombia. Roosevelt offered Colombia a deal for the canal zone, and Colombia's senate said no.

**[VISUAL]** USS Nashville ship to Colón. Panama separates, new Panama flag.
So Roosevelt found another way. When rebels in Panama declared independence, an American warship, the USS Nashville, was already waiting off the coast. Colombian troops could not get through. Within days, the United States recognized the new country.

**[VISUAL]** Canal Zone strip appears with the US flag. Price tag $10,000,000. Stamp "FOREVER".
Two weeks later, a treaty was signed. And the man who signed for Panama was not even Panamanian. He was a Frenchman from the old canal company. America got a strip of land ten miles wide, from ocean to ocean, forever. Panama got ten million dollars, plus a small yearly payment.

**[VISUAL]** Roosevelt bubble "I TOOK THE ISTHMUS". Colombia price tag $25,000,000 in 1921.
Roosevelt later bragged about it: I took the Isthmus. Years later, in 1921, the United States quietly paid Colombia twenty-five million dollars to make peace.

### 5. The Big Ditch

**[VISUAL]** "1904". Construction photo. Mosquito icons disappear one by one.
This time, America started with the mosquitoes. Army doctor William Gorgas drained swamps, fumigated houses and covered water barrels. Within a few years, yellow fever was gone from the zone.

**[VISUAL]** Locks icon. Gatun Lake fills. Ship lifted 85 FEET.
And the engineers changed the plan. Instead of cutting all the way down to sea level, they built a giant staircase of water. Locks lift every ship eighty-five feet up into an artificial lake, carry it across the country, and lower it back down on the other side.

**[VISUAL]** Worker routes from Barbados and the Caribbean. Gold coin vs silver coin icons.
Most of the people who dug it were not American. Tens of thousands came from the Caribbean, especially from Barbados. And the zone had two payrolls. White Americans were paid in gold. Black Caribbean workers were paid in silver, earned far less, and lived in separate camps.

**[VISUAL]** "1914". First ship route. Price tag $375,000,000. Counter 5,600.
It cost about three hundred and seventy-five million dollars, and more than five thousand six hundred workers died. In August 1914, the first ship crossed from the Atlantic to the Pacific. Crossing from one ocean to the other now took about ten hours.

**[VISUAL]** The Canal Zone as a little America: icons of police, school, post office. US flag.
And the Canal Zone became a little piece of America in the middle of Panama. American police, American courts, American schools, even American mail. Panamanians needed permission to cross a strip of land in their own country.

### 6. A Flag Too Far

**[VISUAL]** "1964". Balboa. Flag icons. Bubble "OUR FLAG". Explosion icons.
For decades, that anger grew. Then, in January 1964, Panamanian students tried to raise their flag at an American high school inside the zone. The flag was torn. Riots exploded along the border.

**[VISUAL]** Counter of dead. Panama breaks relations: broken link icon.
When it was over, more than twenty Panamanians and four American soldiers were dead. Panama cut diplomatic ties with Washington. Panamanians still remember that day as Martyrs' Day. And American presidents started to realize the zone was turning into a liability.

### 7. The Giveaway

**[VISUAL]** "1977". Carter portrait. Torrijos with Panama flag. Treaty paper.
In 1977, President Jimmy Carter signed two treaties with Panama's leader, Omar Torrijos. The deal: Panama would take full control of the canal at the end of the century.

**[VISUAL]** Senate vote counter 68 to 32. Stamp "ONE VOTE".
Back home, it was a political firestorm. Ronald Reagan campaigned against it: we bought it, we paid for it, it's ours. The Senate approved the treaties by sixty-eight to thirty-two, just one vote more than it needed.

**[VISUAL]** Shield icon. Neutrality treaty. "1989" troops route.
But there was a catch. A second treaty said the canal must stay neutral, and it gave the United States the right to defend it. And Washington used its muscle. In 1989, twenty-seven thousand American troops invaded Panama to remove the dictator Manuel Noriega.

**[VISUAL]** "1999". Zone turns into Panama flag. Price tag $0.
Then, at noon on December 31st, 1999, control passed to Panama. After eighty-five years, the canal belonged to Panama. For nothing.

### 8. Why Now

**[VISUAL]** Ships routes through the canal. Money flowing to Panama. $5 BILLION A YEAR.
And Panama has run it well. Every single day, dozens of giant ships cross it. The canal brings Panama around five billion dollars a year. And the biggest customer, by far, is the United States.

**[VISUAL]** Drought: Gatun Lake shrinks. Ships queue. Price tag $4,000,000.
But the canal has a weakness: water. Every ship that crosses uses around fifty million gallons of fresh water from the lake. In 2023, a drought dried it up. Crossings were cut, more than a hundred ships waited in line, and one company paid nearly four million dollars just to skip the line.

**[VISUAL]** China flag at both ends: Balboa and Cristobal ports. Trump bubble.
Then there are the ports. Since 1997, the docks at both ends of the canal have been run by a company from Hong Kong. Washington says that gives China a foothold on America's doorstep. And that is the real reason the canal is back in the headlines.

**[VISUAL]** BlackRock deal arrow. China vs USA tug of war.
In 2025, the Hong Kong company agreed to sell those ports to a group led by the American giant BlackRock, as part of a deal worth nearly twenty-three billion dollars. Beijing pushed back, and the deal turned into a tug-of-war between the two superpowers.

**[VISUAL]** Nicaragua route dashed, China flag. Stamp "NEVER BUILT".
And China has looked at this region before. In 2013, a Chinese businessman won the rights to dig a rival canal through Nicaragua, more than three times longer than Panama's. It was never built. But it showed how valuable this narrow strip of land still is.

### 9. The Pattern

**[VISUAL]** Wide map: Panama, Greenland lit up. Chokepoint icons.
Look at the map again. Greenland in the north. Panama in the south. Both sit on the routes that move the world's trade and the world's navies. Control those points, and you control who can move, and how fast.

**[VISUAL]** Back to Panama, Panama flag. Bubble "OUR CANAL".
America built the canal with its money and its engineers, and it gave it back because holding it had become more expensive than letting it go. Panama took it, and made it work. And Panama still says what its students said in 1964: the canal is ours. Now the question is whether a promise signed in 1977 still holds in a world where China is the other superpower.
