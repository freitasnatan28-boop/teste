Making toys and hideouts from cardboard is cheap and fun, but you are building something an animal will scratch, chew and sleep in. The materials you choose matter more than the design. This guide covers what we use, what we avoid, and why.

The general rule: **use as little as possible, keep it away from the surfaces your pet touches most, and always let it dry or cure completely.**

> **Important:** expanding polyurethane glues (for example, the original Gorilla Glue and similar products) are dangerous if swallowed. They can expand in the stomach into a hard mass that may need surgery. Never use them on pet projects, and store them where pets cannot reach. If your pet eats any, call your vet immediately.

## Glue

| Glue | Verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| White PVA glue (school glue, craft glue) | ✅ Our first choice | Water-based, low odor, non-toxic when dry. Slow to dry: allow 24 hours. |
| Wood glue (PVA type) | ✅ Good | Stronger than school glue. Use the water-based PVA kind. |
| Flour and water paste | ✅ Good for light jobs | Edible ingredients, but weak and can attract pests. Fine for papier-mâché style projects. |
| Hot glue | ⚠️ Use carefully | Sets fast and is inert once cool, but forms thin "strings" and hard blobs that pets can pull off and swallow. Trim all strings. Burns are a risk for you. |
| Glue sticks | ⚠️ Too weak | Fine for paper, not strong enough for structures. |
| Super glue (cyanoacrylate) | ❌ Avoid | Can bond skin and fur; not needed for cardboard. |
| Expanding polyurethane glue | ❌ Never | See the warning above. |
| Spray adhesives, solvent-based contact cement | ❌ Avoid | Strong fumes and solvents. |

Wherever you can, **glue the parts your pet won't touch**: bottoms, seams on the outside, inside a frame.

## Paint

You do not need to paint anything. Plain brown cardboard is fine. If you want color:

- **Use water-based, non-toxic paint**, such as children's tempera or acrylic craft paint. In the US, art materials carrying the ACMI "AP" seal have been evaluated as non-toxic for people. That does not make them food, so treat them as safe for outside decoration, not something to be chewed.
- **Paint the outside only.** Never paint the inside of a hideout, the edges of a door, or a scratching surface.
- **Let it dry for several days** with good ventilation before your pet uses it.
- **Avoid spray paint, oil-based paint, varnish and old paint** (older paints may contain lead).
- **Skip glitter, sequins and loose decorations.** They come off and get swallowed.

Markers are an easy alternative for drawing bricks, faces or patterns on the outside.

## Tape

- **Paper tape (kraft tape or masking tape)** is our default. It is easy to tear, and it is paper rather than plastic.
- **Plastic packing tape** holds well, but pets can chew it off and it does not break down if swallowed. Use it only on the outside where your pet cannot reach, or not at all.
- **Duct tape** has a strong adhesive and a fabric layer. Avoid it on anything a pet can chew.
- **Remove all old tape and labels** from boxes before you start.

## The cardboard itself

- **Use plain brown corrugated cardboard.**
- **Remove every staple and metal clip.** Check twice.
- **Avoid glossy, waxed or plastic-coated boxes**, such as some produce boxes and frozen food packaging.
- **Avoid boxes that held chemicals**, cleaning products, pesticides or medication.
- **Avoid boxes with food residue**, especially from foods toxic to pets, such as chocolate, grapes, raisins, onions or anything with xylitol.
- **Recycle anything damp or moldy.**

## Extras: catnip, silver vine and scents

- **Catnip and silver vine** are commonly used in cat toys and are generally considered safe in normal amounts. Not every cat responds to catnip.
- **Never use essential oils** on cat projects. Many essential oils, including tea tree, eucalyptus, citrus and peppermint oils, are known to be toxic to cats. Dogs can also be sensitive to them.
- **Avoid scented sprays and air fresheners** on anything your pet will use.

## Tools

Keep your tools as safe as your materials:

- Use a sharp blade. Dull blades slip and cause more accidents.
- Cut on a cutting mat or an old board, pulling the knife away from your body.
- Work away from your pets, and put blades, glue guns and paint away before your pet comes to inspect the project.

## The final check

Before giving your pet any homemade project, ask:

1. Are all staples, tape and labels gone?
2. Is every glue and paint fully dry?
3. Is there anything small that could be pulled off and swallowed?
4. Is it stable enough that it won't fall on my pet?
5. Will I be around the first few times my pet uses it?
