Brazil is bigger than the contiguous United States, and most first-time visitors try to see too much of it. Flights between regions are long, and a beach in the north can be in a completely different season from a city in the south. The best trips pick two or three of the places below and give each one time.

We live in the Southeast, so a few of these are a weekend away for us. Others take a full day of travel even from São Paulo. The order is roughly how strongly we would recommend each one for a first visit.

## 1. Rio de Janeiro

There is no other big city set like this one: granite peaks dropping straight into the Atlantic, with forest between the neighbourhoods. UNESCO lists Rio's landscape itself, "between the mountain and the sea", as World Heritage. Go up Sugarloaf Mountain by cable car at the end of the afternoon, take the train through the Tijuca Forest to the Christ the Redeemer statue, and spend the rest of the time on the beaches of Copacabana, Ipanema and Leblon.

Give Rio at least four days. The weather on Corcovado changes quickly, and the statue is often in the clouds.

**Tip** Book the Corcovado train for early morning. The light is better, the queues are shorter, and clouds tend to build later in the day.

## 2. Iguaçu Falls

Around 275 separate waterfalls spread along the border with Argentina, and you can see them from both countries. The Brazilian side, in Foz do Iguaçu, gives the famous panorama in a couple of hours. The Argentine side has longer walkways that take you right to the edge of the Devil's Throat. Do both, on separate days.

**Tip** Bring a poncho or a waterproof bag for your phone. The spray near the Devil's Throat soaks everything.

## 3. Salvador

Salvador was Brazil's first capital and is the centre of Afro-Brazilian culture. The colourful Pelourinho district, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is full of baroque churches, and the city's food, music and capoeira are unlike anywhere else in the country. Try acarajé from a street stall and spend an evening watching a drumming rehearsal.

**Tip** Pelourinho is busy and well policed during the day, but quieter streets nearby empty out at night. Use a ride-hailing app after dark.

## 4. Lençóis Maranhenses

White sand dunes stretch for kilometres along the coast of Maranhão, and after the rainy season, thousands of blue and green lagoons fill the hollows between them. It looks like a desert, but it isn't: it rains a lot here in the first half of the year, and that rain is what makes the lagoons. Most visitors base themselves in Barreirinhas or the smaller village of Atins.

**Tip** Go between June and September, when the lagoons are fullest. Outside that window they can be shallow or dry.

## 5. Fernando de Noronha

This volcanic archipelago, around 350 km off the northeast coast, has some of the clearest water in the South Atlantic, spinner dolphins, sea turtles and beaches that regularly top international rankings. Most of it is a protected marine park, and the number of visitors is controlled.

That protection makes Noronha expensive. Every visitor pays a daily environmental fee, and entry to the national park is charged separately.

**Tip** Book flights and accommodation months ahead, and check the current fees on the island's official site before you budget.

## 6. The Pantanal

The Pantanal is the largest tropical wetland in the world, and it is the best place in South America to see wildlife in the open: caimans, capybaras, giant otters, hyacinth macaws and, in the north, jaguars along the riverbanks. Lodges in the north are reached from Cuiabá, and in the south from Campo Grande.

**Tip** The dry season, roughly July to October, concentrates animals around the remaining water and is the best time for jaguar trips from Porto Jofre.

## 7. The Amazon, from Manaus

Manaus is a city of two million people in the middle of the rainforest, with a famous opera house, the Teatro Amazonas, built during the rubber boom. Just outside the city, the dark Rio Negro meets the sandy Solimões, and the two flow side by side without mixing for kilometres. From Manaus, jungle lodges up the rivers offer canoe trips, forest walks and nights listening to the forest.

**Tip** Choose a lodge at least a few hours from the city. The further you go, the more wildlife you are likely to see.

## 8. Paraty

Paraty is a small colonial town on the coast between Rio and São Paulo, with cobblestone streets and white churches. Together with nearby Ilha Grande, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site for both its culture and its biodiversity. Days here are for boat trips to islands and beaches in the bay, or walks to waterfalls in the Atlantic Forest behind the town.

**Tip** The streets in the historic centre flood at high tide on purpose; it was part of the town's design. Wear sandals, not good shoes.

## 9. Bonito

Bonito, in Mato Grosso do Sul, is famous for rivers so clear that floating down them feels like snorkelling in an aquarium. There are also caves, sinkholes and waterfalls. Tourism here is tightly organised: each attraction has a daily limit, and visits are booked through accredited local agencies.

**Tip** In high season, the most popular river trips sell out. Book through an agency before you arrive.

## 10. Florianópolis

Florianópolis is mostly an island, with dozens of beaches: surf beaches facing the ocean, calm bays facing the mainland, dunes, lagoons and fishing villages. It is the most relaxed of the big beach destinations, and a good base for the South.

**Tip** The South has proper seasons. The sea is warm from December to March; in winter it is cold, but the island is quiet and good for hiking.

## Planning your trip

**How long?** Two weeks is enough for three places. A classic first route is Rio, Iguaçu and either Salvador or Paraty. Add the Pantanal or the Amazon if you have more time.

**Getting around.** Distances are large and long-distance trains are almost non-existent, so most visitors fly between regions. Domestic flights are often cheaper when booked well ahead.

**Entry rules.** Visa requirements depend on your nationality. Citizens of some countries, including the United States, Canada and Australia, now need an electronic visa to visit Brazil. Check the official rules with Brazil's [Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://www.gov.br/mre/en) before you book.

**When to go.** The best time depends on the region. We explain it month by month in our guide to [the best time to visit Brazil](/best-time-to-visit-brazil/).

**Safety.** Brazil is a rewarding place to travel, and most visits pass without problems, but petty theft is common in big cities. Our guide to [staying safe in Rio de Janeiro](/is-rio-de-janeiro-safe/) applies to most large cities.

Official tourism information: [Visit Brasil](https://visitbrasil.com/en/).
