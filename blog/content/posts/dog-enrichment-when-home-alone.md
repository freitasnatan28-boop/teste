Most dogs spend some time alone while their people work, study or run errands. Some sleep through it. Others chew the door frame, bark for hours or empty the trash. The right preparation can make alone time much easier, but it is important to choose activities that are safe without supervision, and to recognize when the problem is more than boredom.

## First: boredom or separation anxiety?

These look similar but need different help.

**Boredom** often looks like chewing, digging or getting into things, usually in a relaxed way, and often after the dog has been alone for a while. Enrichment and exercise usually help.

**Separation-related problems** are driven by distress. Signs can include:

- barking, howling or whining that starts soon after you leave and continues;
- destruction focused on doors and windows (exit points);
- house-soiling in a dog that is otherwise house-trained;
- pacing, panting, drooling;
- frantic greetings when you return;
- not eating food left for them while you are out.

If this sounds like your dog, enrichment alone is unlikely to solve it, and punishment will make it worse. Talk to your vet first, since medical issues can play a part, and ask about a referral to a veterinary behaviorist or a qualified, reward-based trainer.

A simple test: film your dog for the first 30 minutes after you leave. The footage often makes the difference clear.

## Before you leave

- **Exercise.** A good walk with plenty of sniffing before you leave helps many dogs settle.
- **Toilet break.** Right before you go.
- **A calm exit.** Keep departures low-key. Big emotional goodbyes can wind dogs up.
- **A comfortable spot** with water, a bed and a comfortable temperature.

## What is safe to leave with your dog

Only leave items your dog has used **under supervision many times** without destroying or swallowing pieces. For many dogs, that might include:

- a sturdy rubber food toy designed for dogs (stuffed with part of their food);
- a durable chew of the right size for your dog, approved by your vet;
- a lick mat made for unsupervised use, if your dog does not chew it.

Check every toy regularly for damage and throw away anything that is breaking apart.

## What NOT to leave unsupervised

- **Cardboard puzzles, snuffle boxes and paper toys.** We love them, but they are for supervised play. A bored dog alone may eat the cardboard instead of just shredding it. Read [Can dogs eat cardboard?](/can-dogs-eat-cardboard/)
- **Rope toys** and anything with strings that can be swallowed.
- **Small balls** or toys that could block the throat.
- **Rawhide and hard chews** you haven't tested with your dog.
- **Anything with batteries** or small parts.

## Better ways to use DIY puzzles

Since cardboard puzzles need supervision, use them **around** alone time:

- **Before you leave:** a 10-minute [snuffle box](/diy-snuffle-box-for-dogs/) or [cardboard puzzle](/diy-dog-puzzle-toys-from-cardboard/) session tires the brain and helps many dogs rest.
- **When you come home:** a quick game of [find it](/teach-your-dog-find-it/) gives your dog an outlet for pent-up energy.

## Build alone time gradually

Many dogs cope better when alone time is taught step by step:

1. Practice short absences: step outside for a minute, then come back calmly.
2. Slowly increase the time, keeping it below the point where your dog gets upset.
3. Leave a long-lasting food toy as you go, so leaving predicts something good.

If your dog becomes distressed even during very short absences, that is a sign to get professional help.

## Other options for long days

- A dog walker or a trusted neighbor at midday
- Doggy daycare, for social dogs
- Coming home at lunch, when possible

How long a dog can be left alone depends on age, health and personality. Puppies and seniors usually need more frequent breaks.

## The bottom line

Exercise before you go, safe and tested toys while you're out, and supervised cardboard games before and after. If your dog shows signs of distress rather than boredom, a vet and a qualified behavior professional are the right next step.
