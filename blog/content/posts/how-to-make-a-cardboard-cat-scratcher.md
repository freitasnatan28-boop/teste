Store-bought cardboard scratchers are just strips of corrugated cardboard glued on edge inside a frame. That means you can make one from the boxes in your recycling bin, in any size you want, for almost nothing. The trick is cutting the strips the right way so the scratching surface is the textured edge of the cardboard, not the smooth face.

This guide walks you through a flat scratcher about 18 inches (45 cm) long, which is long enough for most cats to stretch out fully while they scratch.

## Why cats need something to scratch

Scratching is not bad behavior. Cats scratch to shed the old outer layer of their claws, to stretch the muscles of their back and shoulders, and to leave scent and visual marks from glands in their paws. A cat with no good place to scratch will pick one for itself, and that is usually the sofa.

Giving your cat several approved scratching spots is one of the simplest things you can do for an indoor cat. The [AAFP and ISFM Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1098612X13477537) list the opportunity to perform natural behaviors, including scratching, as a core need for cats kept indoors.

## What you need

- 2 or 3 large corrugated shipping boxes (single-wall is fine and easier to cut)
- A shallow box or tray for the frame, or one extra box to make your own
- A utility knife with a fresh blade, plus a metal ruler and a cutting mat or old board
- Water-based white glue (PVA school glue or wood glue) or a hot glue gun
- A pencil
- Optional: dried catnip or silver vine

Check the boxes before you start. Pull out every staple, peel off plastic tape and shipping labels, and skip any box that is glossy, waxy, damp, or that held cleaning products. Our guide to [choosing cardboard for pet projects](/best-cardboard-for-pet-projects/) covers this in more detail.

## Step 1: Find the direction of the flutes

Look at the cut edge of a box panel. You will see a wavy layer sandwiched between two flat sheets. Those waves are the **flutes**, and they run in one direction across the panel, like the grain of wood.

For a scratcher, you want each strip to expose that wavy edge along its full length. That means cutting **across** the flutes, not along them.

![Diagram showing strips cut across the flutes of a cardboard sheet, then packed on edge in a frame](/assets/img/scratcher-diagram.svg "Cut across the flutes so every strip shows the wavy edge, then pack the strips on edge.")

## Step 2: Cut the strips

Mark lines 2 inches (5 cm) apart across the panels, perpendicular to the flutes. Cut along the lines using a metal ruler as a guide. Several light passes with a sharp blade give a cleaner edge than one hard push, and they are safer.

Strip height matters:

- **2 inches (5 cm)** is the sweet spot. Tall enough to last, short enough to stay rigid.
- Taller strips flex and tear when a cat digs in.
- Shorter strips wear through quickly.

You will need more strips than you expect. A frame 18 by 9 inches (45 by 23 cm) takes roughly 60 to 80 strips, depending on the thickness of your cardboard. Cut them all to the inside length of your frame.

## Step 3: Build or prepare the frame

The frame holds everything tight. Two options:

1. **Use a shallow box**, such as the tray a case of canned drinks came in. Trim the sides to the same height as your strips.
2. **Make your own** by cutting a base and four side walls 2 inches (5 cm) tall, then gluing the walls to the base. Let it set before you fill it.

## Step 4: Pack and glue

Run a line of glue along the bottom of the frame, then stand the strips on edge, wavy side up, packing them tightly against each other. Add glue to the base in sections as you go so it does not dry before the strips are in place.

Keep going until the frame is full and the last strips have to be pushed in. **Tight packing is what makes the scratcher last**, because the strips support each other. If the last gap is too small for a whole strip, squeeze in a strip cut slightly thinner.

Only glue the bottom edges and the ends that touch the frame. Never put glue on the top surface your cat will scratch.

## Step 5: Let it dry completely

Leave the scratcher somewhere your cat cannot reach for at least 24 hours, or until the glue is fully dry. Hot glue sets in minutes, but check for thin glue "strings" and pull them off, because cats can swallow them.

## Getting your cat to use it

A new scratcher in the wrong place will be ignored. A few things help:

- **Put it where your cat already scratches**, such as next to the sofa arm or the door frame they keep attacking. You can move it gradually later.
- **Place one near where your cat sleeps.** Many cats scratch and stretch right after waking up.
- **Sprinkle a little catnip** into the grooves. Not every cat reacts to catnip. Many cats that ignore it respond to silver vine instead.
- **Drag a wand toy across it.** Cats often dig their claws in while chasing.
- **Reward with a treat** when they use it.

Avoid picking up your cat and pressing its paws onto the scratcher. Most cats find that unpleasant and it can put them off the scratcher entirely.

## Variations

**Angled ramp.** Glue the finished scratcher onto a wedge made from a folded box so it sits at about 30 degrees. Some cats prefer an incline.

**Round scratcher.** Glue strips end to end into one very long strip, then roll it tightly like a cinnamon roll, gluing as you go. Wrap the outside with a final strip of plain cardboard.

**Vertical scratcher.** If your cat scratches doors and furniture legs, mount a scratcher on the wall at the height of your cat's full stretch, or make a tall version and fix it firmly to something heavy. A vertical scratcher that wobbles or falls over will scare a cat away from it.

## How long does it last?

That depends on your cat. A heavy scratcher may shred one in a few weeks; others last for months. When the surface is worn down to a dip in the middle, you can either flip it over (if you made it without a base), or throw it in the recycling and make a new one in an hour.

Vacuum the shreds now and then. Most cats do not eat scratcher debris, but if yours chews and swallows cardboard, swap to a sisal post and talk to your vet, since eating non-food items can have medical or behavioral causes.

## Common mistakes

- **Cutting with the flutes instead of across them.** You get smooth strips that tear instead of a textured surface.
- **Packing loosely.** Strips wobble and fall out within days.
- **Using too much glue on the surface.** It creates hard, slippery patches cats avoid.
- **Using a box with tape or staples still attached.** Always strip boxes first.

## Quick FAQ

**Is corrugated cardboard safe for cats?** Plain brown corrugated cardboard is widely used for commercial cat scratchers. Use clean boxes without coatings, tape or staples, and use water-based glue. See our [guide to safe glue, paint and tape](/safe-glue-paint-and-tape-for-pet-diy/).

**Can I use a glue stick?** It is too weak to hold the strips once a cat starts digging in. Use PVA glue or hot glue.

**My cat ignores it. Now what?** Try a different spot, a different angle, and silver vine. Some cats simply prefer vertical scratching. Read [12 indoor cat enrichment ideas](/indoor-cat-enrichment-ideas/) for more ways to tempt them.
