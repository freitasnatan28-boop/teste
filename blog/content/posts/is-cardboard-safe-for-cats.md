Cardboard is everywhere in cat care: scratchers, hideouts, toys and the boxes cats choose for themselves. Most cats scratch it, sleep in it and occasionally nibble the edges without any trouble. But "my cat chews cardboard" can mean very different things, from harmless play to a habit worth a vet visit.

> **If your cat has swallowed string, yarn, ribbon, tinsel or thread, or you can see string coming from their mouth or bottom, do not pull it. Call your veterinarian right away.** String is far more dangerous for cats than cardboard.

## Scratching and sleeping on cardboard

These are normal and generally safe. Commercial cat scratchers are made of plain corrugated cardboard for good reason: it is soft enough for claws, cheap to replace, and most cats love it. Lying in boxes is just as normal. We explain the science in [why cats love boxes](/why-do-cats-love-boxes/).

To keep cardboard play safe:

- remove staples, plastic tape and labels;
- avoid glossy, waxed or printed boxes and boxes that held chemicals;
- use water-based glue and let it dry fully;
- recycle anything soggy, moldy or falling apart.

Our [guide to pet-safe glue, paint and tape](/safe-glue-paint-and-tape-for-pet-diy/) covers materials in detail.

## Nibbling vs eating

**Nibbling** the edge of a box, leaving small damp shreds on the floor, is common, especially in young cats. Many cats seem to enjoy the texture. Usually little or nothing is swallowed.

**Eating** cardboard, meaning actually swallowing pieces regularly, is different. Small amounts of plain cardboard often pass, but swallowing larger pieces can, like any non-food item, cause stomach upset or a blockage.

## Warning signs to call the vet

Contact your veterinarian promptly if your cat has eaten cardboard or another non-food item and shows any of these:

- vomiting, especially more than once;
- not eating;
- hiding more than usual, or lethargy;
- a painful belly;
- straining in the litter box, or no stool;
- drooling, or pawing at the mouth.

Cats are good at hiding illness, so subtle changes matter.

## Why string is the bigger danger

For cats, the most important risk around homemade toys is not the cardboard. It is **linear objects**: string, yarn, ribbon, elastic, dental floss, tinsel and thread.

When a cat swallows a long, thin object, one end can get caught (often around the base of the tongue or in the stomach) while the intestines keep trying to move the rest along. The intestine can bunch up along the string. Veterinarians call this a **linear foreign body**, and it is an emergency that often needs surgery.

That is why our projects:

- never use string or ribbon as decoration;
- tie fabric toys with tight knots and trim loose threads;
- tell you to put wand toys away after play.

If your cat has swallowed string, **do not pull it out from either end**, because that can injure the intestines. Call your vet immediately.

## Why do some cats eat non-food items?

Eating things that are not food is called **pica**. In cats it can involve fabric, wool, plastic, paper or cardboard. Possible factors include:

- **Boredom** and a lack of outlets for hunting and chewing;
- **Stress** or changes in the home;
- **Breed**: wool-sucking and fabric-eating are reported more often in some breeds, such as Siamese and Burmese;
- **Medical causes**, including dental problems, gastrointestinal disease, and other conditions that a vet should rule out;
- **Diet**: some cats eat non-food items when they are hungry or their diet does not suit them.

Because medical causes are possible, a cat that regularly swallows non-food items should see a vet before you treat it as a behavior problem.

## How to reduce chewing

1. **Book a vet check** to rule out dental pain or illness.
2. **Add enrichment**: daily play sessions, [food puzzles](/diy-cat-food-puzzles/) and new climbing and hiding spots. See [12 indoor cat enrichment ideas](/indoor-cat-enrichment-ideas/).
3. **Offer safe things to chew**, such as cat grass grown in a pot.
4. **Swap materials** for a chewer: sisal scratching posts and fabric beds instead of cardboard.
5. **Put boxes away** when you are not around to supervise.

## The bottom line

Scratching and sleeping on plain cardboard is generally safe and good for cats. Occasional nibbling is common. Regularly swallowing cardboard, or any interest in string, needs more attention, and a vet visit is the right first step.

*This article is general information, not veterinary advice. If you are worried about your cat, call your vet.*
