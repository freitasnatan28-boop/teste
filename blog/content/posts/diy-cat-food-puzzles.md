In the wild, a cat makes many small hunting attempts each day, and food has to be found and worked for. Indoors, it appears in a bowl twice a day. Food puzzles bring back a little of the "work" part of eating: your cat has to find, paw out or knock loose their food.

Veterinary behavior researchers have written about this in detail. A review in the *Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery*, [Food puzzles for cats: Feeding for physical and emotional wellbeing](https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X16643753), describes food puzzles as a way to increase activity and give indoor cats an outlet for natural foraging behavior. The [AAFP feeding consensus statement](https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X18791877) also recommends feeding methods that let cats express hunting and foraging behaviors.

You do not need to buy a puzzle feeder to start. These five are made from cardboard you already have.

## Before you start: three rules

1. **Count the food.** Kibble or treats used in puzzles are part of your cat's daily food, not extra. Take the amount out of their regular meal so your cat does not gain weight.
2. **Start easy.** A puzzle that is too hard teaches your cat that puzzles are pointless. Your cat should succeed within the first minute the first few times.
3. **Supervise the first sessions** and recycle any puzzle that gets chewed apart.

Dry food works best for cardboard puzzles. Wet food will soak the cardboard, so keep it for bowls or washable puzzle toys.

## 1. The open box (easiest)

Take a shallow box, such as a shoe box lid or a tray, and scatter a few pieces of kibble inside. Add a few crumpled balls of plain paper so your cat has to nose around them.

**Make it harder:** add more paper balls, or use a box with higher sides.

## 2. The paper roll pyramid

Collect 6 to 10 empty toilet paper rolls. Stand them upright, close together, inside a shallow box and glue the bottoms in place. Drop one or two pieces of kibble into some of the rolls.

Your cat has to reach in with a paw and scoop the food out, which is very close to how cats fish prey out of holes.

**Make it harder:** use taller kitchen paper rolls cut in half, or put food in only a few of the rolls so your cat has to check each one.

## 3. The egg carton

Use a clean cardboard egg carton without any broken egg or residue. Put a piece of kibble in some of the cups. Leave the lid open at first.

**Make it harder:** close the lid and cut a few paw-sized holes in it, about 1.5 inches (4 cm) wide, so your cat has to reach in blind.

## 4. The rolling treat tube

Take a toilet paper roll, fold one end flat and tuck it in. Cut 3 or 4 holes along the side, each slightly larger than a piece of kibble. Put a few pieces inside, then fold and tuck the other end.

As your cat bats the tube around, food falls out of the holes. This one suits playful cats who like to chase.

**Make it harder:** make the holes smaller so the food comes out less often.

## 5. The hole box (hardest)

Take a closed box about the size of a shoe box. Cut several round holes in the top and sides, each about 1.5 to 2 inches (4 to 5 cm) wide: big enough for a paw, too small for a head. Drop kibble inside through a hole.

Your cat has to reach in, feel around, and drag the food out to the edge. Some cats work out that tipping the box is faster. That is fine. Problem-solving is the point.

**Make it harder:** put a few paper balls inside so the food hides behind them.

## How to introduce puzzles

- Start with puzzle 1 or 2, using your cat's favorite treats instead of plain kibble.
- Show your cat a piece of food going in, then step back.
- If your cat walks away, make it easier rather than giving up.
- Offer puzzles at times when your cat is normally active, often early morning and evening.
- Rotate between two or three puzzles so they stay interesting.

## Which cats should skip these?

- **Cats that eat cardboard.** Use washable plastic or silicone puzzles instead.
- **Cats on prescription diets** or with medical conditions that affect eating. Ask your vet before changing how they eat.
- **Very senior cats or cats with arthritis** may find paw-reaching puzzles hard. The open box is the gentlest option.
