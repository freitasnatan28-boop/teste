Cats prefer it much warmer than people do, and in winter you will see them hunting for heat: on top of the fridge, in the sun patch, pressed against a radiator. A simple box bed with good insulation lets them stay warm in a safe spot of your choosing.

Cardboard is a good insulator, which is one reason cats like boxes in the first place. We cover the temperature science in [why cats love boxes](/why-do-cats-love-boxes/).

## What you need

- A box your cat can curl up in, with sides at least 5 to 6 inches (13 to 15 cm) high
- Extra flat cardboard for insulation layers
- An old wool or fleece sweater, or a fleece blanket
- A small cushion or folded towel
- PVA glue or paper tape

Size the box so your cat fits snugly when curled up. A box that is too big loses the "cozy" factor and holds heat less well.

## Step 1: Cut a low entrance

Cut one side down to about 3 to 4 inches (8 to 10 cm) high so your cat can step in easily. Leave the other three sides tall to block drafts. Smooth the cut edge with the back of a spoon.

For older or arthritic cats, cut the entrance lower, about 2 inches (5 cm), so they don't have to climb.

## Step 2: Add a double floor

Cold comes up from the floor. Cut two or three pieces of flat cardboard to fit the bottom of the box and glue them in, stacked. Each layer traps a little air and makes the bed warmer.

If the box will sit on tile or stone, also stand it on a folded towel or a piece of carpet.

## Step 3: Line the walls

Cut extra pieces of cardboard to fit inside the three tall walls and glue them in place. This doubles the insulation where drafts hit.

## Step 4: The sweater trick

Slide the box into the body of an old sweater so the box is "wearing" it, with the neck opening over the entrance. Tuck the sleeves inside to make a soft rim. Then put a cushion or folded towel on the floor.

No sweater? Line the box with fleece. Something that already smells of you or your cat is best.

Trim loose threads and avoid knitted items that are unraveling; long threads are dangerous if a cat swallows them.

## Where to put it

Placement matters more than anything else:

- **In a warm spot**, such as a sunny window or a room you heat, away from drafts and doorways.
- **Off cold floors**, ideally raised on a low, stable shelf or chair if your cat likes height.
- **Near, but not on, heat sources.** A safe distance from radiators is fine. Never place a cardboard bed against a heater, fireplace, stove or space heater: cardboard and fabric can catch fire.
- **Away from the food bowl and litter box.**

## Heating products: what to avoid

It is tempting to add heat, but be careful:

- **Never use a human electric heating pad or electric blanket** in a pet bed. They can get too hot and cats may chew cords.
- **Avoid hot water bottles and microwavable heat packs** unless they are made for pets and used exactly as instructed. Overheated packs can burn, and leaks soak the bed.
- **Pet-specific heated pads** exist; if you use one, follow the manufacturer's instructions, protect the cord, and make sure your cat can move off it to a cooler area.

For most indoor cats, good insulation and a warm location are enough.

## Senior and thin cats

Older cats, very thin cats and hairless breeds often feel the cold more. If your senior cat suddenly seeks heat much more than usual, mention it at the next vet check, as weight loss and some illnesses can make cats feel colder.

## Outdoor and community cats

This bed is for indoor use. Cardboard soaks up moisture and falls apart outside, and wet bedding makes animals colder. Outdoor shelters for community cats need waterproof materials, and straw rather than blankets for bedding, because straw stays dry. Local cat welfare groups can advise on the best design for your climate.

## Keep it clean

Shake out the sweater and towel weekly and wash them regularly, but not all at once if your cat is sensitive to changes in smell. Replace the box once it gets crushed or dirty.
