Most visitors to Rio have a wonderful time and nothing goes wrong. But the city does have real crime, and it is better to arrive with a few good habits than with either fear or false confidence. This is the advice we give friends who visit.

> Before you go, read your own government's travel advice for Brazil. It is updated more often than any blog can be.

## What the real risks are

For visitors, the most common problem by far is **theft**: phones snatched from hands, bags taken on the beach, pickpocketing in crowds and on buses. Violent crime exists, but it is concentrated in areas tourists rarely go. The habits below are mostly about not being an easy target for theft.

## Your phone is the main target

In Rio, a phone in your hand on the street is the single item most likely to be stolen. Snatchers on foot or on bikes are quick.

Step into a shop or a café to check maps. Keep your phone in a front pocket. Many locals carry an older, cheaper phone for the street and leave the good one at the hotel. Turn on the feature that lets you lock and locate the phone remotely before you travel.

## On the beach

Take only what you need: a towel, sunscreen, a little cash and maybe a cheap phone. Don't leave things unattended while you swim; if you are alone, ask the people running the beach kiosk where you rented a chair to keep an eye on your bag, which is a common arrangement. Beaches empty after sunset; don't walk along the sand at night.

## Getting around

Use ride-hailing apps or official taxis, especially at night. The metro is clean and generally safe during the day and in the evening. On city buses, keep bags in front of you.

Know where you are going before you leave, so you are not standing on a corner looking at a map. If a driver's app takes a route through an area that worries you, ask them to go a different way.

## Neighbourhoods

Most visitors stay in Copacabana, Ipanema, Leblon, Botafogo or Flamengo, which are busy and generally fine for visitors during the day. Like any big city, quieter streets empty out at night, and the mood can change from one block to the next.

**Favelas.** Don't wander into a favela on your own, and don't follow a map app that routes you through one. If you want to visit, go with a reputable tour run by people from the community.

**Centro and Lapa.** The historic centre is fascinating by day and very empty at night and on weekends. Lapa is famous for nightlife; go in a group, take a taxi to the door, and keep valuables at the hotel.

## Big events

Carnival, New Year's Eve on Copacabana and big football games bring huge crowds, and pickpockets work them. Carry a copy of your passport rather than the original, a little cash, one card and a cheap phone.

## If something happens

Don't resist a robbery. Hand over what is asked for; things can be replaced. Afterwards, go somewhere safe and contact your card companies. For an official police report, which you may need for insurance, Rio has a special police station for tourists (DEAT) in Leblon. Your embassy or consulate can help with a stolen passport.

Emergency numbers in Brazil: **190** for police, **192** for an ambulance (SAMU) and **193** for the fire brigade.

## The short version

Leave the good phone and the passport at the hotel. Check maps indoors. Use apps for taxis at night. Stay out of favelas unless you are on a community tour. Do those four things, and you have dealt with most of the risk.
