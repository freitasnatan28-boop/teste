"Find it" is the foundation of almost every scent game on this site. Once your dog knows that the words mean "there is something to sniff out, go get it", you can use them to turn meals into games, calm your dog down on walks, and burn energy on days you are stuck indoors.

It is also one of the easiest cues to teach, because dogs are already experts at finding food. You are just giving the game a name.

## What you need

- Small, smelly treats, or part of your dog's daily kibble
- A quiet room to start
- Five minutes

## Stage 1: The treat at your feet

1. Stand with your dog in front of you.
2. Say "Find it!" in a happy voice, and **immediately** drop a treat right by your feet, where your dog can see it fall.
3. Your dog eats the treat.
4. Repeat 8 to 10 times.

That's the whole first lesson. Your dog is learning that "Find it" means a treat is about to appear on the floor.

## Stage 2: A short toss

Say "Find it!" and toss the treat a short distance to the side, so your dog has to move and look for it. Mix left and right. After a few sessions, toss it onto a rug or into slightly longer grass, where your dog has to use their nose rather than their eyes.

## Stage 3: Hide it while they watch

Ask your dog to sit and stay, or have someone gently hold them. Let them watch you place a treat behind a chair leg or under the edge of a cushion. Go back, then say "Find it!"

If your dog doesn't have a solid stay yet, put them behind a baby gate or a closed door for a few seconds instead.

## Stage 4: Hide it out of sight

Now hide the treat while your dog can't see you. Start with very easy spots in the same room. When they find it, celebrate.

Gradually make it harder:

- hide several treats at once;
- use different heights (on a low step, on a box);
- hide treats in another room;
- hide them inside a [snuffle box](/diy-snuffle-box-for-dogs/) or [cardboard puzzle](/diy-dog-puzzle-toys-from-cardboard/).

## Stage 5: Find the toy

Some dogs love searching for a favorite toy as much as food. Use the same steps with the toy, and reward the find with a game of tug or a throw.

## Common mistakes

**Making it too hard too soon.** If your dog gives up, go back a stage. Success keeps them keen.

**Pointing and helping.** It is tempting to point at the treat. Try to stand still and let your dog work it out. If you have to help, make the next round easier instead.

**Saying the cue and then hiding the treat.** In the early stages, the treat should appear right after the words, so your dog connects them.

**Long sessions.** Five minutes is plenty. Stop while your dog still wants more.

## Ways to use "find it" every day

- **Mealtimes:** scatter part of the meal on a clean floor or lawn and say "Find it!"
- **Walks:** a few treats scattered in the grass can help an excited or worried dog focus and settle.
- **Visitors:** "find it" gives your dog a job instead of jumping on guests.
- **Rainy days:** treasure hunts around the house, part of our [10 indoor games for dogs](/indoor-games-for-dogs/).
- **Senior dogs:** a gentle, low-impact way to stay active. See [enrichment for senior dogs](/enrichment-for-senior-dogs/).

## A note on reactive or anxious dogs

Many trainers use scatter feeding to help dogs calm down or refocus. If your dog is fearful or reactive toward people or other dogs, work with a qualified, reward-based trainer or a veterinary behaviorist, who can fit games like this into a proper plan.

## Food safety reminder

Use treats and food that are safe for dogs, avoid chocolate, grapes, raisins, onions and anything containing xylitol, and count treats as part of the daily total so your dog stays at a healthy weight.
