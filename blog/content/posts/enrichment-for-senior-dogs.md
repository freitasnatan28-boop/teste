Older dogs slow down physically, but most still want a job to do. Long runs and fetch may no longer be possible, yet a senior dog's nose usually works long after their legs have stiffened. Gentle enrichment keeps their days interesting and gives you more good time together.

> **Start with a vet check.** Many older dogs live with pain from arthritis, dental disease or other conditions, and pain makes dogs less willing to play. Ask your vet which activities are right for your dog and whether pain relief could help.

## What changes as dogs age

Every dog ages differently, but common changes include:

- **Stiff joints and weaker muscles**, making stairs, jumping and slippery floors harder;
- **Reduced eyesight or hearing**;
- **Less stamina**, so shorter activity sessions suit them better;
- **Changes in thinking and memory** in some dogs. Signs can include confusion, getting stuck in corners, changes in sleep, or forgetting house training. Mention these to your vet.

Mental activity is something you can still offer generously, as long as it is adapted.

## Golden rules for senior enrichment

1. **Short and often.** Three 5-minute sessions are better than one 20-minute session.
2. **Floor level.** Put puzzles on the floor, or raise them slightly for dogs with neck problems, so no one has to jump or stretch.
3. **Good footing.** Play on rugs or yoga mats, not slippery floors.
4. **Easy wins.** Start every game at a level your dog will solve.
5. **Watch for tiredness.** Panting, lying down, or losing interest means it's time to stop.

## 1. Sniff walks

Instead of covering distance, let your dog walk slowly and sniff wherever they like. A 15-minute "sniffari" can be more satisfying for an older dog than a long, brisk walk. Choose flat routes and avoid hot pavement.

## 2. Snuffle box meals

Serve part of a meal in a [DIY snuffle box](/diy-snuffle-box-for-dogs/). Use a box with low sides so your dog doesn't have to stretch, and keep most food near the top at first.

## 3. "Find it" on easy mode

The [find it game](/teach-your-dog-find-it/) is perfect for seniors. Hide treats at floor level, within one room, in places your dog can reach without stepping over anything.

**For dogs with poor eyesight:** use strong-smelling treats, and keep furniture in its usual place so your dog can move around confidently.

**For deaf dogs:** teach a hand signal for "find it", such as a sweeping hand toward the floor.

## 4. Easy cardboard puzzles

The simplest [cardboard puzzle toys](/diy-dog-puzzle-toys-from-cardboard/) work well: a paper roll with the ends loosely folded, a few treats under crumpled paper in a shallow box, or a muffin tin with a few balls. Skip anything that requires a lot of force or pawing.

## 5. Lick and chew time

Licking and gentle chewing can be calming. A lick mat with a thin layer of dog-safe food, or a suitable chew your vet approves (dental health matters here), can fill a quiet half hour. Check that chews suit your dog's teeth.

## 6. Learn a new (easy) trick

Old dogs really can learn new tricks. Choose ones that don't strain the body:

- **Touch** (nose to your hand)
- **Chin rest** (resting their chin in your palm, useful for vet visits)
- **Look** (making eye contact on cue)
- **Which hand?** (guessing which hand holds a treat)

## 7. New smells

Bring the outside in: a few safe items with new smells, like a leaf from a different park or a cardboard box from the grocery store, give an older dog something new to investigate. Avoid essential oils, which can be harmful to dogs.

## 8. Time with you

Gentle grooming, a slow massage of large muscles (if your dog enjoys it), or simply sitting together on a porch watching the world go by are all forms of enrichment. For many seniors, company is the best part.

## Make the home senior-friendly

- Put rugs or runners on slippery floors.
- Use ramps or steps for sofas and cars.
- Raise food and water bowls slightly if your vet suggests it.
- Keep a night light on for dogs with poor eyesight.
- Give them a quiet bed away from busy areas, where they can rest undisturbed.

## When to call the vet

Contact your vet if your senior dog suddenly loses interest in food or games, seems confused, has trouble getting up, cries out when moving, or changes their sleep pattern. Many of these changes can be helped when caught early.
