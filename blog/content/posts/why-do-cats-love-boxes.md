You buy your cat a new bed, and they sleep in the box it came in. It is one of the most familiar cat jokes on the internet, and it turns out there are good reasons behind it. Researchers have looked at boxes and stress, temperature, and even the odd habit of sitting inside squares drawn on the floor.

## 1. Boxes help cats cope with stress

The best-known study on this comes from Utrecht University in the Netherlands. Researchers followed 19 cats newly arrived at an animal shelter. Half were given a hiding box in their cage and half were not. The cats with boxes had lower stress scores in the first days and [adapted to their new environment faster](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2014.09.002) than cats without a box.

Why would this be? Cats tend to deal with stress by avoiding it. Faced with something new or worrying, they hide and watch rather than confront it. A box gives them a safe place to do exactly that.

**At home**, this means a box or hideout is especially useful when:

- you have moved, or rearranged the house;
- there are visitors, children or other new animals;
- there are loud noises, like fireworks or building work;
- your cat has just come home from the vet or a cattery.

## 2. Boxes are warm

Cats like it warmer than we do. The temperature range in which a cat does not have to spend extra energy to keep warm (the "thermoneutral zone") is often cited as roughly 86 to 100 °F (30 to 38 °C), based on figures published by the US National Research Council. That is much warmer than most living rooms.

Cardboard is a good insulator, and a small enclosed space traps body heat. Curling up in a snug box is an energy-efficient way for a cat to stay warm, which may explain why cats so often choose boxes that look far too small for them.

## 3. Boxes are a perfect ambush spot

Cats are ambush predators. Rather than chasing prey over long distances, they wait in cover and pounce. A box with an opening lets a cat watch the room while staying mostly hidden, which suits that hunting style perfectly. Many cats will also use a box as a launch point in play, darting out at a toy or another cat.

## 4. Cats sit in "boxes" that aren't even there

This is the strangest finding. In 2021, researchers ran a citizen science experiment called [If I fits I sits](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2021.105338). Cat owners placed shapes on the floor at home, including a square outlined with tape and a "Kanizsa square", an optical illusion made of four Pac-Man-like shapes that suggest the corners of a square without any lines connecting them.

Cats chose to sit inside the illusory square about as often as inside the real taped square, which suggests that cats may perceive the illusion the way humans do. It also suggests that what attracts cats is not only physical walls, but the *idea* of an enclosed space. The study was small and done in homes rather than a lab, so the researchers treated it as a first step rather than proof.

**Try it yourself**: put a square of tape on the floor and see whether your cat sits in it.

## 5. New things smell interesting

A box that just arrived carries smells from warehouses, trucks and other people. Cats investigate new objects by smelling and rubbing on them, which also leaves their own scent behind from glands on their cheeks. Part of the appeal of a new box may simply be that it is something new to explore and claim.

## How to use this at home

Understanding why cats like boxes helps you give them better versions:

- **Give at least one hiding place per cat**, plus one extra in multi-cat homes. A [one-box cat house](/diy-cardboard-cat-house/) is enough.
- **Put a box somewhere warm**, such as near a sunny window, with a soft T-shirt inside.
- **Offer height too.** Many cats feel safest when they can hide *and* look down. A [cardboard cat castle](/cardboard-cat-castle/) gives both.
- **Never pull a cat out of a hiding place** to make them socialize. Let them come out when they are ready.
- **Refresh boxes regularly.** Cardboard wears out, and a new box is new enrichment.

## When hiding is a warning sign

Hiding in a box is normal. But a cat that suddenly hides much more than usual, stops eating, or avoids the litter box may be unwell or in pain. Cats are good at hiding illness, so changes in hiding behavior are worth mentioning to your vet.
