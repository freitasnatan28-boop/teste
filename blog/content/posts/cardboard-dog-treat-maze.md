A treat maze is a shallow box divided into channels. You drop a treat in, and your dog has to nudge it with their nose or paw through the gaps to the opening where they can grab it. It slows down fast eaters, works the brain, and takes a sharp dog a few minutes instead of a few seconds.

## Who this suits

- **Dogs who enjoy puzzles but don't eat cardboard.** A determined chewer will simply destroy the maze. For those dogs, use rubber puzzles and read [Can dogs eat cardboard?](/can-dogs-eat-cardboard/)
- **Small and medium dogs.** Large dogs can enjoy it too if they use their nose rather than flattening the box with a paw. Use double-wall cardboard for them.

## What you need

- A shallow box or tray with sides about 2 to 3 inches (5 to 8 cm) high. The lid of a large shipping box, or a drinks tray, works well.
- Extra cardboard for the inner walls (double-wall is best)
- Utility knife, metal ruler, pencil
- PVA glue (let it dry fully) or a hot glue gun, used carefully
- Dry treats or kibble that roll or slide easily

Remove staples, tape and labels first. See [the best cardboard for pet projects](/best-cardboard-for-pet-projects/) for where to find strong boxes.

## Step 1: Plan the channels

Each channel must be wide enough for the treat to move and for your dog's nose to push it, but narrow enough that your dog can't just scoop the treat out:

| Dog | Channel width |
|---|---|
| Small dog | 1.5 to 2 in (4 to 5 cm) |
| Medium dog | 2 to 2.5 in (5 to 6 cm) |
| Large dog | 2.5 to 3 in (6 to 8 cm) |

Sketch a simple path on the base: start in one corner, zigzag across, and finish at an exit where the treat drops into an open "reward pocket" your dog can easily reach.

## Step 2: Cut the walls

Cut strips of cardboard the same height as the box sides. Make each strip slightly longer than you need and trim it to fit.

## Step 3: Glue the walls in place

Glue the walls along the bottom edge only, following your sketch. Leave gaps at the turns so the treat can pass from one channel to the next. Let the glue dry completely before your dog sees the maze. If you use hot glue, pick off every thin glue string.

## Step 4: Make the reward pocket

At the exit, leave an open square area with no walls, about the size of your dog's muzzle. This is where the treat ends up and your dog gets paid.

## Three difficulty levels

1. **Beginner:** a short path with two turns. Put the treat close to the exit the first few times.
2. **Intermediate:** a longer zigzag. Start the treat at the far end.
3. **Advanced:** cover part of the maze with a cardboard "roof" with a few nose holes, so your dog has to push the treat along partly blind.

You can also tilt the maze slightly by putting a book under one end. The treat rolls downhill, which helps beginners.

## Teaching your dog to use it

1. Show your dog the treat going into the maze, close to the exit.
2. Let them sniff and nose it out. Cheer them on.
3. Move the starting point further from the exit each time.
4. If your dog tries to pick up the whole maze or starts chewing the walls, end the game calmly and try an easier level later.

Don't solve it for them, but don't let them get frustrated either. A good session ends while your dog is still keen.

## Safety notes

- **Always supervise.** This is a game, not a toy to leave with your dog.
- **Use safe treats** only, never chocolate, grapes, raisins, onions or anything containing xylitol.
- **Count the treats** as part of your dog's daily food.
- **Recycle the maze** once the walls are wobbly, chewed or greasy.
